Pharmacology

Methadone's two clocks

24–36 hWithdrawal suppression
6–8 hPain relief

The gap between the clocks can leave a person in pain while still covered against withdrawal.

Counselors do

  • Map the person's day: doses, pain, sleep, work.
  • Help them name the problem for their prescriber.
  • Listen without assuming misuse.

Counselors do not

  • Change, split, or advise on doses.
  • Judge a pain report as drug-seeking.
  • Promise a medical outcome.

Education only. See Module 1 and the methadone half-life journal piece ↗.

Safety

Opioid overdose: first response

  1. Check. Cannot wake, slow or no breathing, blue or grey lips, gurgling sounds.
  2. Call emergency services (911 in the US).
  3. Naloxone. Give it as directed on the package. Nasal spray goes in one nostril.
  4. Breathe. If trained, give rescue breaths. Breathing is the emergency.
  5. Position. If breathing, place them on their side. Stay.
  6. Re-check. If no response after 2–3 minutes, give another dose if you have one. Naloxone can wear off before the opioid does, so the person still needs medical care.

General public-health guidance. Take a naloxone training and carry naloxone.

Ethics

Scope of practice

In scope

  • Listening, education, and skills practice.
  • Preparing someone for a prescriber visit.
  • Referral and warm handoff.
  • Facilitating peer circles.

Out of scope

  • Prescribing or adjusting medication.
  • Diagnosing pain conditions.
  • Promising confidentiality in a safety emergency.
  • Acting as someone's only support.
Referral

Refer promptly for

  • New or worsening weakness or numbness
  • Changes in bladder or bowel control
  • Severe new headache, vomiting, or confusion, especially in someone with spina bifida or a shunt
  • Thoughts of suicide or of harming others
  • Signs of overdose or severe withdrawal

When unsure, ask a clinician. Same-day medical evaluation is the safe default for sudden neurological change.

Interviewing

Asking about pain

  • "Tell me about a typical day with your pain."
  • "What does relief look like for you, and when does it stop?"
  • "What have you tried, and what happened?"
  • "What do you wish the people treating you understood?"
  • "What would you like me to do, and not do?"

Dialogic interviewing (Turney, 1997; Smith, 2025).

Peer circles

A circle on one card

  1. Arrive. Light start, settle in.
  2. Agree. Read agreements, check in. Passing is fine.
  3. Share. Own experience, no advice on medication.
  4. Reflect. The witness says what they heard.
  5. Commit. One small optional step.
  6. Close. Round and crisis numbers.

If someone is in danger

Peer circles and this protocol are not emergency services. In the United States, call or text 988 (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline) or call 911 for a medical emergency. SAMHSA's National Helpline is 1-800-662-4357 (free, confidential, 24/7). Carry naloxone where opioids are present and learn how to use it. Outside the US, use your local emergency number.
