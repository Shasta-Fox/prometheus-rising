About the protocol

What is the Prometheus Rising Protocol?

A restorative harm reduction and counselor education program. It connects the pharmacology of opioid treatment to the lived realities of recovery and chronic pain, and it trains counselors and peer mentors to support people managing opioid dependence. It also runs free peer circles where accountability stays in the community instead of turning into punishment.

Start with the Framework or the ten-minute walkthrough.

Who is it for?

Counselors, counselors in training, peer mentors, and people with lived experience of recovery or chronic pain who want to support others. Peer circles are open to anyone who agrees to the circle agreements.

Is it free?

Participation in drop-in circles and mutual aid groups is permanently free. Full tuition and credentialing costs are covered for First Nations applicants and for people facing financial disruption.

Who is it dedicated to?

Jonathan M. Martin (1984–2024). The dedication honors lived wisdom and mutual aid.

How does this relate to Star Nootropics?

Prometheus Rising is now its own project. It grew out of the author's independent research and journal writing, and that work remains in the Star Nootropics research archive ↗. The protocol and its community live here.

The research

Where is the research behind the protocol?

The central paper is Steady State: Methadone, Chronic Pain, Spina Bifida, and the Psychology of Recovery. It reviews methadone maintenance, the high burden of chronic pain in methadone programs, the pharmacological mismatch between pain relief and withdrawal protection, and spina bifida as a case that brings these problems into focus. It closes by proposing an online training platform for counselors, which became this protocol.

The paper also appears in the Star Nootropics research archive ↗. Every source used across the protocol, with DOI and Google Books links, is on the Sources page.

Why do pain relief and withdrawal protection run on different clocks?

Methadone's elimination half-life at steady state averages 24 to 36 hours, so one daily dose can prevent withdrawal for a day. Its pain relief is much shorter, about three to six hours when treatment begins and roughly 8 to 12 hours with repeated dosing. A person with chronic pain can be protected from withdrawal and still spend part of the day in pain. The paper reviews the evidence, including Toombs and Kral (2005) and Baxter et al. (2013), in its section on the half-life mismatch.

Does the research say methadone works?

The paper reviews decades of evidence that methadone maintenance lowers mortality and keeps people in care, including a meta-analysis of cohort studies (Sordo et al., 2017). It also reviews what is still unknown. For example, no study it reviewed directly tested divided methadone dosing for people with chronic pain in opioid treatment programs, and the evidence for methadone in neuropathic pain is very limited. Its limitations section lists the rest.

Is the paper a systematic review?

No. It is a narrative synthesis, and its sources vary in design, setting, and date. The training platform it proposes is a proposal, and its effects have not been tested.

Safety and scope

Is this treatment or medical advice?

No. Everything here is education. It does not diagnose, treat, or replace care from a prescriber, doctor, or counselor.

Should I change my methadone dose because of what I read here?

No. Never change a dose on your own. If your pain returns before your next dose, bring a clear description of your day to your prescriber. Module 1 teaches counselors to help you prepare that conversation.

What do counselors and peer mentors do, and not do?

They listen, educate, practice skills, refer, and facilitate circles. They do not prescribe, adjust medication, diagnose pain conditions, or act as someone's only support. See the scope of practice card.

What if someone is in crisis or having an overdose?

Call emergency services. In the United States, call or text 988 or call 911. SAMHSA's National Helpline is 1-800-662-4357. The overdose card has the first-response steps and the circle guide has the crisis path.

Peer circles and community

How do the peer circles work?

Each session follows the same shape: arrive, agree, share, reflect, commit, close. Everything is optional and passing is always allowed. The circle guide includes a timer you can set to your session length.

Can I stay anonymous?

Yes. Nicknames and camera-off are normal choices. Confidentiality has limits: serious risk of harm to someone may require outside help, and facilitators say so at the start.

What happens when someone is hurt in a circle?

The group slows down and follows the harm-response steps: pause, hear each person separately, name the impact, ask what is needed, agree on repair, and follow up. Removal is for safety only and is reviewed by a second steward. See the steps.

Supporting the work

You can contribute through PayPal from the support section. Contributions help fund free peer circles, scholarships for First Nations and low-income learners, and open curriculum development.

Do I need to donate to take part?

No. Peer circles and scholarships are free whether or not anyone donates, and contributions do not shape what the protocol says.

Learning

How long is the curriculum, and is there a credential?

Seven modules over twelve weeks. Learners who complete all seven with their assessments reviewed receive a certificate of completion. Outside accreditation is a goal, not a current claim. See the Curriculum and the Implementation plan.

Why restorative and anti-oppressive approaches?

Shame and exile are common reasons people stop reaching out, and labels like "drug-seeking" can be a harm in themselves. The Framework explains how restorative justice, anti-oppressive practice, trauma-informed care, transformative learning, and harm reduction fit together.

If someone is in danger

Peer circles and this protocol are not emergency services. In the United States, call or text 988 (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline) or call 911 for a medical emergency. SAMHSA's National Helpline is 1-800-662-4357 (free, confidential, 24/7). Carry naloxone where opioids are present and learn how to use it. Outside the US, use your local emergency number.
