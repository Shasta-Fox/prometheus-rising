Part 4 · Implementation

From Protocol to Program

A program lasts when its vision is clear, its values show up in daily decisions, and the people running it plan for change. This page states the vision using the structure of Collins and Porras (2005): core ideology, envisioned future, ambitious goals, and vivid description.

Core ideology

Purpose. To prepare counselors and peer mentors to support people living with opioid dependence and chronic pain, and to keep accountability and care inside the community.

Values.

  • Compassion. Every person receives empathetic, individual care.
  • Integrity. Ethical standards in every interaction, including scope of practice and honest evidence.
  • Commitment to learning. Continuous development for counselors, mentors, and the program itself.
  • Collaboration. Teamwork and open communication, with power shared between clinicians and people with lived experience.
  • Innovation. New methods and tools for training and support, tested before adopted.
  • Access. Permanent free peer support and full scholarships for First Nations and low-income learners.

Envisioned future

A training model that is accessible, structured, and effective, built on trained human facilitators and interactive video conferencing. Facilitators give personalized guidance, and video lets trainees connect with experts and each other across regions. Hybrid design combines online sessions with in-person workshops so that people with different learning preferences are included.

Long-range goals

These are draft targets for the steering group to confirm, adjust, or replace. Collins and Porras (2005) call goals of this kind "big, hairy, audacious": ambitious, long-term, and motivating.

GoalMeasure of progress
Train counselors and peer mentors at scale over five yearsCohorts started and completed, with the number set by the steering group
Build a mentorship network connecting experienced professionals with traineesActive mentor pairs, with check-ins at one, three, and six months
Pursue an accredited certification for substance-use counselorsSteps completed with the chosen accrediting body
Combine video conferencing with hands-on workshopsIn-person workshops held each year and learner ratings of both formats
Keep peer circles permanently freeShare of funding secured without fees to participants

Vivid description

  • Counselors and mentors in lively video sessions, learning in real time from experienced professionals and from people who have lived what is being taught.
  • A learning community where practitioners share what worked, what failed, and what they are still unsure about.
  • Refresher courses and expert-led question sessions that keep skills current.
  • A network of skilled counselors across regions, each one able to hear a pain complaint and a recovery story in the same breath.

Phased roadmap

Phase 1 · Months 0–6

Foundation

Form the steering group with equal voice for lived experience and clinical expertise. Finalize the circle agreements, harm-response steps, and steward onboarding. Pilot Modules 1 and 2 with a small cohort.

Phase 2 · Months 6–18

First full cohorts

Run the full twelve weeks twice. Gather learner and mentor feedback, revise, and publish an evaluation summary. Launch the scholarship fund.

Phase 3 · Months 18–36

Network and hybrid

Start the mentorship network, add in-person workshops in at least two regions, and begin conversations with an accrediting body.

Phase 4 · Years 3–5

Scale and sustain

Expand cohorts, train facilitators to run their own, and review the program against its values.

Supporting change

Success depends on how well the organization adapts to innovation and change (Palmer et al., 2017). Five changes carry most of the weight.

ChangeWhat the program does
Leadership engagementLeaders join sessions, model the agreements, and take part in their own professional development (Palmer et al., 2017).
Resistance to changeSome counselors will hesitate about online learning. Offer structured onboarding, coaching, and mentorship to ease the move (Mansour, 2024).
Culture of continuous learningKeep an open feedback loop so the training stays relevant (Cameron & Quinn, 2011).
Technology supportReliable help for connection problems, a low-bandwidth option, and phone-only access to voice rooms.
Hybrid designCombine online and in-person formats so different learners are served (Pisano, 2019).

Evaluation

Evaluation is designed with participants, not imposed on them (Smith, 2025). Four sources of information are collected in simple, consented form:

  • Self-anchored scales built by each learner and revisited at weeks 6 and 12.
  • Practice observation of facilitated circles by two reviewers, one with lived experience.
  • Participation data such as attendance and retention, used to find barriers and never to rank people.
  • Harm-response review: how many cases, how long they took, and whether the repair held.

The program reports what it finds, including what did not work. It does not claim clinical outcomes for participants. Findings from other settings, such as Brady's (2019) consultation in Cyprus, show that a single site and a short timeline limit what can be concluded, and the same caution applies here.

Governance and money

  • The steering group includes people with lived experience of recovery and chronic pain, First Nations representation, and clinicians. Decisions that affect participants are made with them.
  • Contributions support curriculum and research and do not shape conclusions.
  • Financial reports are shared with participants each year.
