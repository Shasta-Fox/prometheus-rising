Part 2 · Curriculum

Seven Modules, Twelve Weeks

A cohort-based program for counselors, counselors in training, and peer mentors. Each week combines a short reading, one live session in the voice room, and a practice task between sessions. Peer mentors with lived experience co-teach every module.

How a week works

Read

20–30 minutes. A short reading or a journal piece.

Gather

90 minutes. A live session using the circle format.

Practice

One task between sessions, done alone or in pairs.

Reflect

A short written or spoken reflection. Always optional to share.

Learners who finish all seven modules with their assessments reviewed receive a Prometheus Rising certificate of completion. Accreditation with an outside body is a goal, not a current claim (see Implementation).

Modules

Framework →
Module 1Weeks 1–2

The Pharmacology of Lived Experience

Methadone's two clocks: withdrawal suppression lasts 24 to 36 hours, analgesia lasts 6 to 8 hours. What that gap feels like at 3 a.m.

Learning outcomes

  • Explain in plain language why maintenance coverage and pain relief run on different clocks.
  • Describe how this gap can be mistaken for misuse.
  • Name what falls outside a counselor's scope: dosing, tapering, and prescribing.

Practice activity

Pairs map one day of a person's medication timeline and mark where pain, withdrawal, work, and sleep collide. Then they write the question the person could bring to a prescriber.

Reflection prompt

What would I assume about someone whose pain returns hours before their next dose, if I had not learned this?

How learning is shown

One-page timeline plus a prescriber-conversation script, reviewed by a peer mentor with lived experience.

Anchor reading

Journal: methadone half-life, pain, and risk

Module 2Weeks 3–4

Dismantling the Single Story

How the label "drug-seeking" makes chronic pain invisible, and how to practice with ambiguity instead of suspicion.

Learning outcomes

  • Identify the single stories that shape clinical and community responses to pain and opioid treatment.
  • Practice responses that stay curious when a self-report cannot be verified.
  • Use dialogic interviewing: explore social location and difference as a cooperative inquiry (Turney, 1997; Smith, 2025).

Practice activity

Role-play three versions of the same first meeting: one driven by suspicion, one by rescue, one by curiosity. The group names what each version does to trust.

Reflection prompt

Which story do I tell about people who use opioids, and who taught it to me?

How learning is shown

Recorded or written role-play with a debrief that names one change in practice.

Anchor reading

Adichie (2009); Freire (2000); Smith (2025)

Module 3Weeks 5–6

Congenital Disability & Lifelong Pain

Adults with spina bifida: complex pain presentations, and telling nociceptive joint strain from neuropathic lesions.

Learning outcomes

  • Describe how lifelong disability changes the history a counselor must listen for.
  • Distinguish, at an educational level, nociceptive joint strain from neuropathic pain, and know when to refer for medical assessment.
  • Identify institutional barriers in healthcare, education, and employment using structural boundary mapping (Smith, 2025).

Practice activity

Learners draw a structural boundary map for one case (composite, not a real person): services, funding, transport, housing, and the places each blocks access.

Reflection prompt

Where does this person's pain end and the system's failure begin?

How learning is shown

Boundary map plus three referral questions. A clinician with disability expertise gives feedback.

Anchor reading

Journal: restoring the floor; the floor, not the ceiling

Module 4Weeks 7–8

Peer Mentorship & Communitas

People with lived experience of both chronic pain and opioid recovery become mentors. The community moves from liminality toward communitas.

Learning outcomes

  • Describe liminality and communitas and how a group moves between them (Buechner et al., 2020).
  • Use narrative: share a story without turning it into a performance or a prescription (Kroth & Cranton, 2014).
  • Set mentor boundaries: what peers share, what they hold back, and when to bring in a facilitator.

Practice activity

Learners facilitate a short story circle using the Peer Circles format and receive feedback from a mentor-in-training and a facilitator.

Reflection prompt

What can I offer from my own story, and what belongs only to me?

How learning is shown

Facilitated circle observed by two reviewers, one with lived experience.

Anchor reading

Buechner et al. (2020); Palmer (2000)

Module 5Weeks 9–10

Trauma, Culture & Cumulative Harm

Awareness, safety, trust, collaboration, choice, and empowerment. Cumulative, historical, and colonial trauma. Consulting without paternalism.

Learning outcomes

  • Apply the six trauma-informed principles to a program design (Brady, 2019).
  • Explain cumulative and historical trauma and how they appear in recovery settings (Kira, 2010; Mitchell, 2019; O'Neill et al., 2018).
  • Examine Western clinical bias and avoid paternalistic approaches (Brady, 2019; Amari, 2026).

Practice activity

Learners audit one program (their own workplace, a clinic, or the Discord server itself) against the six principles and propose one change that increases choice.

Reflection prompt

Whose way of knowing has my training treated as default?

How learning is shown

Written audit with one proposed change and a plan to ask the people affected.

Anchor reading

Brady (2019); Mitchell (2019); Hailes et al. (2021)

Module 6Week 11

Community Accountability & Repair

Restorative responses to harm in online and in-person groups, and holding someone accountable without exiling them.

Learning outcomes

  • Run the harm-response steps: pause, hear, name impact, ask for needs, agree on repair, follow up.
  • Separate accountability from punishment and from silence.
  • Know when safety requires removal and how to do it with dignity.

Practice activity

Learners work through three scenarios (a heated argument, a boundary breach, a relapse disclosure that frightens the group) using the harm-response process.

Reflection prompt

What does repair look like when the person who harmed and the person harmed both need this community?

How learning is shown

Scenario write-up and one facilitated practice round.

Anchor reading

Pointer et al. (2020); Fuller & Russo (2016)

Module 7Week 12

Ethics, Scope & Reflexive Practice

What counselors and peers do and do not do. Referral. Confidentiality. Self-reflection on power, privilege, and limits.

Learning outcomes

  • State scope of practice in your role and where a referral is required.
  • Explain the limits of confidentiality and how to tell participants up front.
  • Keep a reflexive practice that examines your own culture, values, and power (Dominelli, 2012; Houston & Marshall, 2020).

Practice activity

Learners write their own scope statement and a personal reflexivity plan. They share one commitment with the cohort.

Reflection prompt

What will I do when I do not know, and who will I ask?

How learning is shown

Scope statement, reflexivity plan, and a closing circle.

Anchor reading

Hailes et al. (2021); Watts (2004)

Assessment approach

Assessment follows the anti-oppressive principle that learners are co-assessors. Each learner builds a self-anchored scale with a facilitator at the start (for example, "how confident am I responding to a pain complaint without suspicion, 1 to 5") and revisits it at weeks 6 and 12. Reviewers include peer mentors with lived experience. The goal is to show growth in practice, not to rank people.

Scholarships

Full tuition and credentialing costs are covered for First Nations applicants and for people facing financial disruption. No documentation of hardship beyond a short statement is required.

If someone is in danger

Peer circles and this protocol are not emergency services. In the United States, call or text 988 (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline) or call 911 for a medical emergency. SAMHSA's National Helpline is 1-800-662-4357 (free, confidential, 24/7). Carry naloxone where opioids are present and learn how to use it. Outside the US, use your local emergency number.
