    Foundations: three sessions everyone takes

    How a week works →

    Foundations opens every cohort. Volunteers finish here, and peers and clinicians carry it into the curriculum as a shared starting point. Each session follows the curriculum's weekly rhythm: a short reading, a 90-minute live session, one practice task, and a reflection that is always optional to share.

    Foundations · F1Week 1

    What Harm Reduction Is

    Meeting people where they are, with abstinence as one possible goal rather than the price of help.

    Learning outcomes

    • Define harm reduction as a public health practice and as an approach to counseling.
    • Describe the range of goals a person may choose, from safer use to less use to abstinence, and explain why harm reduction does not withhold help from people who keep using (Logan & Marlatt, 2010).
    • Name services harm reduction includes: naloxone distribution, syringe services, drug checking, and medication for opioid use disorder.

    Practice activity

    Rewrite three intake questions so they ask about the person's own goals before assuming abstinence. Trade with a partner and name what each version invites the person to say.

    Reflection prompt

    Where have I seen help withheld from someone because they were still using?

    How learning is shown

    Three rewritten questions, discussed with a partner.

    Readings

    Anchor: Logan & Marlatt (2010). Further: Marlatt & Witkiewitz (2002).

    Foundations · F2Week 2

    Reading the Evidence

    What a trial can show, what a cohort study can show, and what only people's own accounts can show.

    Learning outcomes

    • Tell a randomized trial from an observational study and a qualitative study, and say what each can and cannot establish.
    • Place a claim on the evidence ledger as supported, preliminary, or a hypothesis, and defend the placement.
    • Explain how law and funding decide who can reach treatment, using Spain's change in methadone law as a case (Torrens et al., 2013).

    Practice activity

    In pairs, place five claims on the evidence ledger. Then pick the claim you disagreed about most and name the study that would settle it.

    Reflection prompt

    Which belief about drug use do I hold most firmly, and what kind of evidence is it built on?

    How learning is shown

    Ledger placements with one sentence of reasoning for each.

    Readings

    Anchor: Torrens et al. (2013), free to read. Further: Langendam et al. (2001); Avants et al. (2004); Wilson et al. (2015).

    Foundations · F3Week 3

    First Response and First Conversation

    Recognizing an opioid overdose, knowing your role, and opening a conversation without judgment.

    Learning outcomes

    • Recognize an opioid overdose and walk through the steps on the overdose card: call emergency services, give naloxone, support breathing, and stay with the person.
    • State what you do and do not do in your role, and when to refer, using the scope and referral cards.
    • Use language that describes a person rather than a label, using the language card.

    Practice activity

    Talk through the overdose card's steps with a partner, then role-play handing someone a kit and asking what would help. Anyone who will carry naloxone also takes a hands-on naloxone training from a local health department or harm reduction program.

    Reflection prompt

    What would help me stay steady and present if someone stopped breathing in front of me?

    How learning is shown

    A spoken walk-through of the overdose card and a scope statement in your own words.

    Readings

    Anchor: Little & Franskoviak (2010), with the overdose, scope, referral, and language cards.

    Scope

    This session teaches recognition and response, not medical treatment. In an emergency in the United States, call 911.

    How the price was set

    Community recovery coach trainings cost a few hundred dollars. One host of the 30-hour CCAR Recovery Coach Academy charged $250 to $300 for a virtual cohort in January 2026. In Iowa, the state's peer workforce collaborative covers that training for trainees who complete its application steps, and recovery coaches already on staff are asked whether their employer will pay.

    Trainings that lead to a state license cost far more. Oregon's approved psilocybin facilitator programs averaged $9,359 in tuition, and individual programs list $6,599 and $8,800 before practicum fees. This course leads to a certificate of completion, not a license, so its tuition sits with the community trainings. Its full path runs about 37.5 hours, close to those academies' 30.

    The field's own conferences price by means as well. Harm Reduction International's 2027 conference charges a lower rate to delegates from low- and middle-income countries and to unwaged students and delegates, and it admits volunteers free in exchange for working half the conference.

    Evidence ledger: what we know, and how sure we are

    The course's central claims carry a status that matches the strength of their evidence. A claim moves when new evidence arrives. The course's own effectiveness sits on this ledger too, as a hypothesis.

    Supported

    Several studies or a systematic review point the same way. Still open to revision.

    Preliminary

    Some evidence, but too little, too weak, or from too few places to rely on.

    Hypothesis

    A reasonable idea that no study has tested yet.

    1. Supported

      Methadone treatment lowers the risk of death.

      A meta-analysis of cohort studies found 11.3 deaths per 1,000 person-years among people in methadone treatment and 36.1 among people out of treatment (Sordo et al., 2017).

      Why supported and not settled

      The finding holds across many cohorts, but cohort studies observe people rather than assign them to treatment, so people who stay in treatment may differ in other ways from people who leave. Read it with Langendam et al. (2001) in the Foundations readings.

    2. Supported

      Methadone keeps more people in treatment than flexible-dose buprenorphine.

      A Cochrane review found that methadone retained more patients than flexible-dose buprenorphine (Mattick et al., 2014).

      Why supported and not settled

      The comparison is between two medications, not between methadone and no treatment, and a review is only as strong as the trials it pools.

    3. Supported

      Methadone's protection against withdrawal outlasts its pain relief.

      One daily dose can prevent withdrawal for about a day, while its pain relief wears off within hours. This gap is the core of Module 1 and the two-clocks card.

      Why supported and not settled

      The research paper reviews this from pharmacology sources, including Toombs and Kral (2005) and Baxter et al. (2013). How long relief lasts varies from person to person and lengthens with repeated dosing.

    4. Preliminary

      Admitting people quickly, regardless of ability to pay, expands methadone treatment without harming outcomes.

      In one program's open-access model, the patient census rose 183% over nine years and waits fell from 21 days to same-day admission, without apparent harm to retention, nonmedical opioid use, or mortality (Madden et al., 2018).

      Why preliminary

      The result comes from one program observed over time, not compared with a control. It needs replication elsewhere.

    5. Preliminary

      Methadone relieves chronic neuropathic pain.

      A Cochrane review found only very limited, very low-quality evidence on methadone for chronic neuropathic pain (McNicol et al., 2017).

      Why preliminary

      A plausible mechanism is not evidence that a treatment works. Counselors and peers should not repeat this claim as fact.

    6. Hypothesis

      Dividing the daily methadone dose gives steadier pain relief to people with chronic pain in opioid treatment programs.

      A total daily dose spread across the day may give steadier relief than the same amount taken once, but no study in the research paper's review tested this directly in opioid treatment programs.

      Why a hypothesis

      Divided doses often depend on take-home privileges or program approval, so the question is partly about program rules, not only pharmacology. Counselors and peers never advise on dosing. They help people bring the question to their prescriber.

    7. Hypothesis

      Training with this protocol improves outcomes for the people learners serve.

      The course's design follows from the evidence above, but no one has tested its effects.

      Why a hypothesis

      Each cohort's learner and mentor feedback goes into a published evaluation summary, as the Implementation plan commits. Until that evidence exists, treat this course as a reasoned proposal.

    Sources for each claim are in the reference list of the research paper, Steady State.

    Readings

    Twenty sources the course adds to the program's evidence base, placed where they are taught. Each entry shows its study design, so you can see what kind of evidence it offers before you read it. Anchor readings marked free to read cost nothing. For any other anchor, the facilitator provides a summary in the session.

    What the design tags mean
    Randomized trial
    Assigns people to groups by chance. The strongest test of cause in this list.
    Observational study
    Compares groups as they are found. Shows association, not cause.
    Qualitative study
    Studies people's own accounts. Shows meaning and experience.
    Review
    Summarizes earlier research.
    Economic analysis
    Weighs costs against outcomes.
    Policy or conceptual analysis
    Examines laws, funding, or ideas rather than measuring outcomes.
    Clinical approach or program description
    Describes how practitioners work. Argues from practice rather than testing outcomes.
    Research article
    Design not shown in the source list. Check the paper.

    Citations come from the author's Google Scholar list. Entries marked "Details checked" were matched against a second source to complete missing journal details. Check every entry against the original before academic or clinical use.

    The course ethics code

    These commitments govern the course, and learners can hold the facilitators to them.

    1. Lived experience counts as expertise. People with lived experience co-teach every module, and the course pays them for that work.
    2. Evidence comes before persuasion. Central claims carry their status on the evidence ledger, and facilitators say so when they do not know.
    3. Scope protects people. The course teaches recognition, response, and referral. It never teaches anyone to change a dose, diagnose a condition, or provide treatment.
    4. Every exercise is optional. You may always pass, and no one has to share their own history to take part.
    5. Confidentiality has stated limits. What is said in a session stays there, except where serious risk of harm requires outside help. Facilitators say this at the start, and no one records a session without everyone's consent.
    6. Language describes people, not labels. The course uses the program's language card and asks people how they describe themselves.
    7. Money never buys access to care. Peer circles stay free, scholarships need only a short statement, and contributions never change what the course teaches.
    8. The course is evaluated in public. Learner and mentor feedback goes into a published evaluation summary, and the course changes when the evidence says it should.

    Questions

    Is the program accredited?

    No. Prometheus Rising is not accredited by any outside body, and the course offers no continuing education credits. Completing a path earns a Prometheus Rising certificate of completion, which is not a license or a state certification. Outside accreditation is a goal in the Implementation plan, not a current claim.

    Will these hours count toward my certification or license?

    Do not count on it. Certification boards decide which training they accept. Ask your board before you rely on these hours.

    Can my employer pay?

    Yes. Choose the employer-paid seat when you request a seat, and the team sends an invoice.

    What if I can't pay?

    Foundations is free for everyone. For the curriculum, choose a scholarship seat or a community seat at the amount you can manage. Scholarships cover the full cost for First Nations learners and anyone facing financial hardship, and a short statement is all we ask.

    When and where do sessions meet?

    Live and online, in the program's voice rooms. Cameras are optional. The team sends each cohort's dates and times to everyone who requests a seat.

    Do I have to share my own history?

    No. Every exercise is optional, and you may always pass. Peers who choose to share decide what to share and what to keep.

    What happens after I request a seat?

    The team replies by email with cohort dates. For a paid seat, the reply includes a payment link. For a scholarship seat, it confirms your place. Requesting a seat commits you to nothing.

    If someone is in danger

    Peer circles and this protocol are not emergency services. In the United States, call or text 988 (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline) or call 911 for a medical emergency. SAMHSA's National Helpline is 1-800-662-4357 (free, confidential, 24/7). Carry naloxone where opioids are present and learn how to use it. Outside the US, use your local emergency number.
