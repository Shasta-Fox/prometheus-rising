Why the Protocol Is Built This Way

Counselors learn pharmacology in a classroom and meet people in a different place. The Prometheus Rising Protocol aims to close that gap through five commitments that appear in every module, every circle, and every decision the group makes.

1. Restorative justice: repair before punishment

Restorative justice puts the focus on repairing damage instead of punishing the person who caused it (Pointer et al., 2020). In a recovery community this matters because shame and exile are common reasons people stop reaching out. When someone breaks a circle agreement, the group asks who experienced the impact, what they need, and what the person responsible can do to repair the harm.

Online spaces suit this work in some ways. Participants can hold back their identities and engage in play, which lowers the stakes for trying new ways of resolving conflict. The protocol treats that freedom as a teaching resource and also as a duty of care: anonymity must never be a shield for harm.

In practice

Every circle has a written harm-response process (see Peer Circles). Moderators use it before any removal decision.

2. Anti-oppressive practice: look at the system, not only the person

Anti-oppressive practice (AOP) reads a person's distress in the context of institutional power, discrimination, and inequality instead of as an isolated personal fault (Dominelli, 2017; Baines, 2020). Smith (2025) applies this to community-based mental health and describes tools that fit our work: dialogic assessment interviews, mapping the barriers in a person's institutional environment, and self-report measures that client and practitioner build together.

For Prometheus, this has direct consequences. When clinicians label a person with chronic pain "drug-seeking," they create an institutional barrier, and the label itself can cause harm. AOP also asks counselors to avoid the "expert mystique" and to treat the person as the expert on their own life (Dominelli, 1996; Smith, 2025).

In practice

Module 2 trains counselors to hold ambiguity instead of resolving it through suspicion. Scholarship and access decisions are made with the people affected, not for them.

3. Trauma-informed care: six principles

Brady (2019) describes trauma-informed service delivery through six principles: awareness, safety, trust, collaboration, choice, and empowerment. In her consultation work in a post-crisis system, treatment providers had little formal training in evidence-based trauma interventions even though trauma rates among patients in addiction treatment were high. Cooperation also grew once previously isolated organizations began to talk to each other.

Cumulative trauma and historical loss compound over time (Kira, 2010; Mitchell, 2019; O'Neill et al., 2018). The protocol therefore treats historical and colonial trauma as part of a person's current context, especially for First Nations learners and participants.

PrincipleWhat it looks like in a Prometheus circle or class
AwarenessFacilitators know common trauma responses and name them without diagnosing.
SafetyClear agreements, camera-optional rooms, content notes, and a visible exit and crisis path.
TrustFacilitators do what they say they will do and explain every boundary.
CollaborationFacilitators and participants write circle norms together, and learners co-design assessments.
ChoiceEvery share, exercise, and disclosure is optional. You may always pass.
EmpowermentPeers and learners take on facilitation roles and decide how the group changes.

4. Transformative learning: change that holds

Transformative learning is the process by which people revise deeply held assumptions about themselves and the world. It often follows a crisis or a "disorienting" experience (Cranton & Roy, 2003; Taylor & Cranton, 2012). Communities support the process by providing narrative, dialogue, and a sense of togetherness (Kroth & Cranton, 2014; Buechner et al., 2020). Buechner and colleagues describe movement from liminality, the unsettled middle, toward communitas, a shared bond among people who navigated a transition together. That is the arc Module 4 uses.

Other parts of the protocol draw on specific authors:

  • Critical reflection and dialogue. Freire (2000) frames education as practice of freedom, and hooks (1994) treats it as a way to engage the world critically.
  • The single story. Adichie (2009) warns that one story about a group produces stereotype. Module 2 is built on this.
  • Emotion and imagination. Dirkx (2001) argues feeling is a route to meaning in adult learning, so sessions leave room for feeling.
  • Playfulness and loving perception. Lugones (1990) offers "world-traveling" as a way to understand another person's life without taking it over. Circle games and role-plays follow from this.
  • Hope as a skill. Boler (2003) argues educators are responsible for helping learners build agency, and Macy and Johnson (2022) describe communities that act on hope together.
  • Embodied learning. McDermott (2014) shows the value of physical and emotional engagement. Voice rooms cannot fully provide it, so the protocol recommends in-person gatherings alongside online ones.

5. Harm reduction: meet people where they are

Harm reduction accepts that people will make choices that carry risk and works to lower the harm without requiring abstinence as the price of help. In this program it also means giving accurate pharmacology. Module 1, for example, shows why methadone can suppress withdrawal for 24 to 36 hours while its pain relief wears off after 6 to 8 hours. A counselor who knows this can hear a pain complaint without assuming misuse.

Counselors and peers do not prescribe, adjust, or advise on changing any medication. They help people prepare for conversations with their prescriber, name the problem clearly, and know when to refer.

6. Limits and risks

  • Online is not a replacement for in-person contact. It extends access and offers privacy, and it cannot supply everything a face-to-face community does.
  • Reflection can hurt. Not everyone will have a positive experience, and some people find examining their behavior overwhelming. Facilitators watch for this, keep every disclosure optional, and offer resources.
  • Appropriation of anti-oppressive language. Critics warn that emancipatory ideas can shrink into administrative checkboxes (Wilson & Beresford, 2000; Dominelli, 1998). The protocol answers this with ongoing reflexive practice and with decisions shared with those affected.
  • Evidence. Much of the literature cited here describes frameworks and case-based consultation. The protocol identifies findings that come from a single setting. See sources on the Sources page.
If someone is in danger

Peer circles and this protocol are not emergency services. In the United States, call or text 988 (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline) or call 911 for a medical emergency. SAMHSA's National Helpline is 1-800-662-4357 (free, confidential, 24/7). Carry naloxone where opioids are present and learn how to use it. Outside the US, use your local emergency number.
