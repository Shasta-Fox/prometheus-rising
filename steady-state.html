Steady State: Methadone, Chronic Pain, Spina Bifida, and the Psychology of Recovery

Oct 3, 2026 • @Shasta Fox

Methadone maintenance treatment addresses one question: how can a person with opioid addiction stop cycling between intoxication and withdrawal long enough to rebuild a life? Fifty years of research show that it answers that question well. A second question, however, has followed methadone into clinics for just as long and has received far less attention: what happens when the person in treatment also lives with chronic pain? Chronic severe pain affects more than a third of people in methadone programs (Rosenblum et al., 2003), and some studies place the figure above 60% when researchers count pain of any intensity (Jamison et al., 2000). For people with lifelong physical conditions such as spina bifida, pain is not an occasional complication but a constant companion (Peterson et al., 2022; Werhagen et al., 2010).

This paper examines methadone at the meeting point of addiction and chronic pain. It begins with how addiction works, both in the brain's reward and inhibitory systems and in the psychology of the people who live with it. It then reviews the evidence for methadone maintenance, the high burden of pain in methadone programs, and the pharmacological feature that most shapes the experience of patients with pain: methadone's pain relief fades hours before its protection against withdrawal does. It considers spina bifida as a case that brings these problems into sharp focus, examines how program structures help or hinder patients with pain, and closes with a proposal for an alternative, technology-supported training platform.

The central argument is that methadone can serve people with both addiction and chronic pain well, but only when programs design treatment around two facts they often ignore: the drug's analgesic action is shorter and more variable than its maintenance action, and patients with pain are frequently understood through a single story of drug-seeking that obscures their real needs. Closing these gaps requires changes in dosing practice, in counselor training, and in the structures through which patients and providers learn from one another.

How Addiction Works

The Brain: Reward, Inhibition, and Adaptation

Addictive drugs differ in their chemistry but converge on a common circuit. Tan et al. (2010) summarized a defining feature of addictive drugs: they increase dopamine in the brain's mesolimbic reward system and trigger lasting synaptic adaptations there that may ultimately produce compulsive behavior. Their study showed that even benzodiazepines, which strengthen inhibition, become rewarding by quieting the inhibitory interneurons that normally restrain dopamine neurons. Opioids are generally understood to act through a similar route of disinhibition. The shared lesson is that addiction is not simply the pursuit of pleasure; repeated chemical exposure rewires the brain's reward machinery.

The brain also adapts in the opposite direction. When a drug repeatedly suppresses or amplifies a system, that system changes to restore balance, and the adaptation becomes visible when the drug is withdrawn. Malcolm (2003) reviewed evidence that chronic modulation of the brain's main inhibitory receptor complex plays a major role in the nervous system's dysregulation during alcohol abstinence, and that the same receptors contribute to both reward and withdrawal. For opioids, one of the clearest signs of adaptation involves pain itself. People maintained on methadone are, on average, between 42% and 76% less tolerant of cold-pressor pain than matched controls, a pattern researchers attribute to opioid-induced hyperalgesia, an increased sensitivity to pain produced by long-term opioid exposure (Compton et al., 2008). Addiction and pain therefore share biology: the same exposure that sustains dependence can lower the threshold at which pain is felt.

Pain is also part of how many people enter addiction. In a survey of patients in methadone programs and residential treatment, almost a quarter of methadone patients reported that pain was one of the reasons they first used drugs (Rosenblum et al., 2003). For these patients, recovery and pain management are not separate tasks.

The Psychology of Addiction

Biology explains vulnerability; psychology explains persistence. Watzlawick et al. (2011) argued that the very solutions people attempt maintain many human problems, and that lasting change often requires a shift in how people understand a problem rather than more effort within the old frame. Applied to addiction, the attempted solution to pain, distress, or withdrawal becomes the engine of the problem: each dose relieves suffering briefly and deepens the dependence that produces more suffering.

Emotion sits at the center of this cycle. Dirkx (2001) described emotions and imagination as central to how adults construct meaning from experience, rather than as obstacles to rational learning. People in recovery are not only learning new behaviors; they are revising the meanings they attach to pain, relief, shame, and hope. Cranton and Roy (2003) argued for a holistic view of transformative learning that accounts for these personal, emotional, and relational dimensions, and the same holism applies to recovery.

The stories others tell about people with addiction shape that process. Adichie (2009) warned that a single story creates stereotypes that are not untrue but incomplete, and that the incompleteness does harm. Patients in methadone treatment who report pain often meet a single story: that of the drug-seeking patient. Providers who treat chronic pain in methadone patients describe tremendous ambiguity in deciding whether pain complaints reflect clinical need or a desire for drugs (Berg et al., 2009). That ambiguity is real, but when it hardens into a single story, clinicians can discount a patient's pain precisely because that patient receives treatment meant to help them.

Methadone Maintenance: The Evidence Base

Methadone maintenance provides a long-acting opioid, under clinical supervision, to people dependent on shorter-acting opioids, and it has long been the primary form of treatment for opioid dependence in the United States (Wechsberg et al., 2007). Ten years after introducing the approach, Dole and Nyswander (1976) reviewed a decade of experience across treatment programs in the United States and abroad. The approach grew rapidly and then stalled. Ball and Ross (2012), in their study of patients, programs, services, and outcomes, noted that by the 1980s the great expansion of methadone treatment had ceased, with no new facilities opening in New York City over a ten-year period.

The outcome evidence has remained consistent. Farrell et al. (1994) reviewed more than two decades of research and noted that two authoritative bodies had reported on the benefits of methadone maintenance. Senay (1985) reviewed evidence that employment rates improved among patients in methadone maintenance. The most important outcome is survival: a meta-analysis of cohort studies found all-cause mortality of 11.3 deaths per 1,000 person-years among people in methadone treatment compared with 36.1 among those out of treatment (Sordo et al., 2017). Methadone also keeps people in care; a Cochrane review found that it retained more patients than flexible-dose buprenorphine (Mattick et al., 2014).

Access has been the weak link. Madden et al. (2018) described an open-access model in which staff rapidly enrolled prospective patients regardless of ability to pay and offered real-time access to multiple voluntary treatment options. Over the nine years after implementation, the program's patient census rose 183%, from 1,431 to 4,051, and average wait times fell from 21 days to same-day admission, without apparent harm to retention, nonmedical opioid use, or mortality. The finding matters for this paper because it shows that the structure of a program, and not only the medication it dispenses, determines who benefits.

Chronic Pain in Methadone Maintenance

How Common and How Disruptive

Pain warrants sustained attention in methadone programs. Rosenblum et al. (2003) surveyed representative samples of 390 patients in two methadone programs and 531 patients in 13 residential programs in New York State and found chronic severe pain in 37% of methadone patients and 24% of residential patients. The authors concluded that substance abuse treatment programs need comprehensive, structured pain management programs. Jamison et al. (2000) reported chronic pain in 61% of methadone patients they studied, while cautioning that patients in methadone programs may be especially attentive to physical symptoms. Dunn et al. (2014) confirmed that people receiving methadone report more chronic pain than the general population and characterized its severity and interference with daily life, and Voon et al. (2015) examined pain among high-risk patients in Vancouver, noting calls for more research on the safety of methadone for people with chronic pain.

Pain also tracks with dose. In a study of 170 consecutive patients in Tel Aviv, 55% had chronic pain, and methadone doses rose with both pain severity and pain duration: patients without chronic pain received a mean of 147 mg per day, those with severe or very severe pain about 176 mg, and those with pain lasting more than 10 years about 182 mg (Peles et al., 2005). The direction of this relationship is uncertain. Higher doses may reflect attempts to treat pain, greater tolerance, or both.

The numbers do not capture what pain does to a life. In interviews with methadone patients, Karasz et al. (2004) found that chronic severe pain interfered with work and family life and obstructed the fulfillment of social roles. For a person trying to rebuild stability through treatment, pain can erode the very roles that recovery depends on.

A Lower Threshold for Pain

People maintained on methadone appear to feel some kinds of pain more intensely. Scimeca et al. (2000) noted that methadone maintenance patients as a group show lowered pain thresholds and argued that their pain requires deliberate, informed treatment. Compton et al. (2008) reported that methadone-maintained patients were 42% to 76% less tolerant of cold-pressor pain than matched controls, and that adding the NMDA antagonist dextromethorphan for several weeks did not improve their tolerance, although the response appeared to differ by gender. Zahari et al. (2018) examined the relationship between serum methadone concentration and cold-pressor pain sensitivity and reported that some patients had reached clinical stability at very low blood concentrations, which suggests that blood levels alone do not explain clinical stability or pain sensitivity.

The hyperalgesia may not be permanent. Eyler (2013), reviewing acute and chronic pain among methadone patients, described evidence that former methadone patients showed higher heat pain thresholds than current patients, consistent with recovery of pain sensitivity after treatment ends. For the patient in pain today, however, the practical implication is that standard analgesic doses may be inadequate, and complaints of pain deserve serious evaluation rather than suspicion.

The Half-Life Mismatch: Why Pain Relief Fades Before Withdrawal Protection

Methadone's long elimination half-life is the foundation of maintenance treatment. Once a person reaches steady state, the half-life averages 24 to 36 hours, although reported values range from 4 to 91 hours (Baxter et al., 2013). A single daily dose can therefore prevent withdrawal for a full day.

Pain relief follows a different clock. Methadone's analgesia lasts about three to six hours when treatment begins and extends to roughly 8 to 12 hours with repeated dosing (Toombs & Kral, 2005). A dose that holds withdrawal away for 24 hours may relieve pain for only a third to a half of that time. A full once-daily dose can protect patients with chronic pain from withdrawal while leaving them in pain for much of each day, often worst in the hours before the next dose. This mismatch explains why pain guidance favors dividing methadone into several doses through the day, starting with small doses every eight hours when clinicians prescribe methadone for pain alone (Toombs & Kral, 2005).

Methadone's broader pharmacology adds both promise and risk. Brown et al. (2025) reviewed its action as both an opioid agonist and an NMDA receptor antagonist, a combination that may help with complex and neuropathic pain, while emphasizing its narrow therapeutic window, variable pharmacokinetics, potential to prolong the QT interval, and risk of respiratory depression. The same review described the evidence on whether methadone counters opioid-induced hyperalgesia as conflicting. In cancer pain, clinicians have successfully used methadone for patients already on maintenance therapy (Manfredi et al., 2001), and a 2026 study of opioid rotation in refractory cancer pain suggested that methadone may shorten the time to pain stabilization when patients switch from transdermal fentanyl (Mercadante et al., 2026).

The long half-life also makes change slow and risky. Because methadone accumulates in tissue, a dose increase takes days to reach its full effect, and the first weeks of treatment carry the highest risk of overdose when doses rise too quickly (Baxter et al., 2013). Sedation that appears after a dose increase is therefore a signal that deserves attention rather than a symptom to override with stimulants. Interactions with other medications can raise or lower methadone levels; Weschules et al. (2008) documented both actual and potential drug interactions associated with methadone, including cases in which patients starting an interacting medication needed substantially higher methadone doses to restabilize. Finally, adding other opioids to manage pain in a maintenance patient raises concerns that authors debated in correspondence about methadone maintenance and chronic pain (Portenoy et al., 2003).

For patients with pain, the practical conclusion is that clinicians often need to consider the dosing schedule alongside the total methadone dose. A total daily dose divided across the day may provide steadier pain relief than the same amount taken once, but in most opioid treatment programs a divided schedule depends on take-home privileges or program approval, which brings the structure of treatment into the clinical picture.

Spina Bifida and Chronic Pain

Spina bifida is a developmental defect in which the vertebral column fails to close completely, usually with a corresponding anomaly of the spinal cord (Werhagen et al., 2010). It is present from birth, and its consequences for pain extend across the lifespan. In a cohort of 110 adults with spina bifida, 32% reported pain: 20% experienced nociceptive pain, arising from tissue such as muscles and joints, and 10% experienced neuropathic pain, arising from damage to the nervous system itself (Werhagen et al., 2010). Neuropathic pain was more common in older patients and in those without hydrocephalus, and some patients reported that their pain had worsened because of complications such as urinary tract infection and tethered cord.

Adults with spina bifida also receive more opioids. A study of privately insured adults compared 22,647 people with cerebral palsy or spina bifida with 931,528 adults without these conditions; clinicians prescribed significantly more opioids to those with cerebral palsy or spina bifida, measured in morphine equivalents, across every pain category, including among people with no recorded pain diagnosis (Peterson et al., 2022). The authors called for better pain screening and for matching treatments to underlying pain mechanisms.

These findings make spina bifida a demanding test of methadone treatment. A person with spina bifida and opioid use disorder may carry several kinds of pain at once, nociceptive and neuropathic, each responding differently to treatment. Authors often cite methadone's NMDA receptor antagonism as a reason it may help neuropathic pain (Brown et al., 2025), but the controlled evidence is thin: a Cochrane review found only very limited, very low-quality evidence on methadone for chronic neuropathic pain (McNicol et al., 2017), and adding a separate NMDA antagonist did not improve experimental pain tolerance in methadone-maintained patients (Compton et al., 2008). Trials have not confirmed the theoretical advantage.

Spina bifida also raises practical questions that the pain literature rarely addresses. Mobility limitations, medical appointments, and the physical demands of daily clinic attendance may make the structure of methadone treatment harder to meet for people with lifelong physical disabilities. This is an inference from the general structure of methadone programs rather than a finding of the studies reviewed here, and it identifies a gap that research on disability and opioid treatment should address directly.

The Structure of Treatment: Programs, Rules, and Communities

Safety, Control, and Diversion

Methadone programs operate under rules designed to keep a powerful drug safe. Breslin and Malone (2006) described how one program maintained its viability and safety, including its efforts to prevent diversion, the selling of methadone outside treatment. Supervised dosing, limits on take-home doses, and staff review of dose changes protect patients and communities, but they also determine whether a patient with pain can divide a dose across the day or adjust it quickly. The same structure that protects against diversion can, for a patient with chronic pain, lock a short-acting analgesic effect into a once-daily schedule.

Programs and Communities

Programs shape the lives of the people they serve in ways that extend beyond medication. When British Columbia changed the regulations governing its provincial methadone program, McNeil et al. (2015) found that people receiving methadone in Vancouver had to negotiate new forms of structural vulnerability, and that low-threshold supports, such as methadone delivery, mattered to their ability to stay in treatment. Housing can provide the stability that treatment needs: residents of low-threshold harm reduction housing described gaining the stability and the time to think about medications for opioid use disorder (Chatterjee et al., 2025).

Programs also affect the neighborhoods around them, and the evidence presents a more complex picture than public fears suggest. In Philadelphia between 2007 and 2017, the presence of an outpatient methadone facility reduced property crime by 28.8% and total crime by 17.8% within a 200-meter radius, while drug crime rose by 30.8% and violent crime by 7.0% in that radius; there were no significant effects beyond 200 meters (Moyer & Ridgeway, 2020). Cost matters as well. In jails, methadone maintenance cost between $86 and $185 per patient per week, with larger programs achieving lower per-patient costs (Horn et al., 2018). Researchers in Iran have developed performance management models for methadone centers to address their organizational challenges (Behzadi et al., 2022), a reminder that program quality is a management problem as well as a clinical one.

Counselors and Pain

Counselors stand at the center of the program structure, and they often lack preparation to address pain. In interviews with 30 drug counselors in methadone treatment, Oberleitner et al. (2017) found that counselors without prior training in pain management cited their own lack of expertise, the complexity of patients' needs, concerns about medication regimens, and reliance on patient self-report as barriers; consulting with medical providers was the main facilitator. The authors concluded that counselor training and program design should address these barriers. Berg et al. (2009) offered a complementary recommendation for medical providers: acknowledge the ambiguity of treating pain in people with addiction and engage in open discussion rather than resolving the ambiguity through suspicion. Together, these findings point toward the kind of training this paper proposes.

An Alternative Platform: The Prometheus Rising Protocol

The Original Proposal

The gaps identified above, in counselor knowledge, in how pain is understood, and in how patients and providers learn from each other, suggest a need for new training structures. An earlier proposal by the present author, the Prometheus Rising Protocol, outlined such a structure for Prometheus Care Group: a technology-driven online training program to prepare substance abuse counselors and mentors to support people affected by the opioid crisis (Fox, 2025). The proposal rested on three observations. First, younger generations increasingly seek support, learning, and community online, which creates both risks and opportunities for harm reduction (Fox, 2025). Second, online harm reduction communities allow people to share stories, access resources, and build systems of accountability, with mentors modeling healthy behavior and encouraging critical self-reflection (Cranton & Roy, 2003; Fox, 2025). Third, people displaced by climate-related disasters bring experience that can inform leadership, and digital platforms can re-engage them as peer mentors (Fox, 2025).

The proposal combined e-learning modules, virtual simulations, and real-time feedback to help counselors move between “maps,” the theoretical models of addiction and recovery, and “territory,” the personal narratives of the people they serve. It grounded this work in restorative principles of community accountability (Fuller & Russo, 2016) and in feminist and engaged pedagogy that treats knowledge as co-created rather than delivered (hooks, 1994).

Extending the Platform to Pain

The findings of this paper suggest that the same platform could address the needs of patients with chronic pain in methadone treatment. Four training modules follow directly from the evidence:

Module 1: The Pharmacology of Lived Experience

Teaches the dual clocks of methadone: why pain relief lasts hours while withdrawal protection lasts a day (Toombs & Kral, 2005), why dose increases take days to reach full effect and carry early accumulation risks (Baxter et al., 2013), and how drug interactions alter serum levels (Weschules et al., 2008). Virtual case simulations train counselors to recognize when pain fluctuations warrant clinical discussion regarding divided dosing.

Module 2: Dismantling the Single Story

Utilizes Adichie's (2009) framework to deconstruct how reflexive labels of “drug-seeking” render chronic pain invisible. Counselors practice embracing clinical ambiguity (Berg et al., 2009) to overcome the barriers identified by Oberleitner et al. (2017): lack of expertise, uncertainty around medication regimens, and suspicion of self-reports.

Module 3: Congenital and Lifelong Physical Disability

Prepares counselors to navigate the complex needs of clients with conditions such as spina bifida, differentiating between nociceptive and neuropathic mechanisms (Werhagen et al., 2010) and addressing extensive histories of high-dose opioid exposure (Peterson et al., 2022).

Module 4: Peer Mentorship and Communitas

Engages individuals with lived experience of both chronic pain and opioid recovery as peer mentors, grounding theoretical knowledge in lived narrative. Moving from liminality toward communitas (Buechner et al., 2020), the platform establishes a collaborative, restorative community of mutual accountability.

Learning and Change

The platform's design draws on adult learning theory. Dirkx (2001) emphasized that adults construct meaning through emotion and imagination, which suggests that simulations and narratives will teach more than lectures about pharmacology alone. Boler (2003) argued that educators who unsettle learners' worldviews take on an ethical responsibility to offer hope, a principle that applies to counselors asked to question long-held beliefs about patients who report pain. Palmer (2000) described vocation as a calling people discover by listening to their own lives, and a platform that invites counselors to reflect on why they entered this work may help sustain them in it.

Implementing the platform would involve both incremental and transformational change (Palmer et al., 2017). Incremental change would add pain content to existing counselor training. Transformational change, in the sense Watzlawick et al. (2011) described as a shift in how people understand a problem, would change how programs see patients with pain: as people whose pain is part of their recovery, with needs beyond diversion prevention. Because younger patients and counselors are more likely to engage through digital channels, the platform would also draw on frameworks for engaging young people in health research and services (Sanchez et al., 2024).

Implications for Practice and Research

Practice

Several practical steps follow from the evidence. Methadone programs should assess pain routinely, at intake and throughout treatment, because chronic pain is common and often severe in this population (Rosenblum et al., 2003). When a patient's pain follows the daily rise and fall of methadone's analgesic effect, programs should consider whether a divided dosing schedule is clinically appropriate, recognizing that methadone's pain relief is far shorter than its maintenance action (Toombs & Kral, 2005). Safeguards against diversion remain necessary (Breslin & Malone, 2006), but programs should design them to allow consideration of divided dosing for patients who need it.

Dose changes require patience and monitoring. Because methadone accumulates over days, sedation after an increase, new medications, and changes in other substances deserve close attention (Baxter et al., 2013; Weschules et al., 2008), and clinicians should consider cardiac risk when doses are high (Brown et al., 2025). Counselors should receive training in pain, since its absence is one of the barriers they themselves report (Oberleitner et al., 2017). For patients with lifelong conditions such as spina bifida, pain assessment should distinguish nociceptive from neuropathic pain, because the two may respond differently to treatment (Werhagen et al., 2010).

Research

The evidence also reveals important gaps. No study reviewed here directly tested divided methadone dosing for patients with chronic pain in opioid treatment programs, and the evidence for methadone in neuropathic pain remains very limited (McNicol et al., 2017). Research on people with spina bifida and other lifelong disabilities in methadone treatment appears to be scarce, despite their high rates of pain and opioid prescribing (Peterson et al., 2022). Finally, the platform proposed here would need evaluation: measures could include counselor knowledge and attitudes about pain, patient-reported pain interference, retention in treatment, and whether patients feel that clinicians believe their pain reports, building on the program-level outcomes that open-access models have already tracked (Madden et al., 2018).

Limitations

This paper is a narrative synthesis, not a systematic review, and its sources vary widely in design, setting, and date. Much of the pain research in methadone programs is cross-sectional, so it cannot establish whether pain drives higher doses, higher doses reflect tolerance, or both (Peles et al., 2005). The experimental studies of pain sensitivity use laboratory stimuli such as cold water, which may not capture clinical pain (Compton et al., 2008). The spina bifida studies did not examine people in methadone treatment, so their application to that group is an inference. Several sources, including books and correspondence, were available only in summary form, and this paper describes their contributions at that level. Finally, the training platform is a proposal; its design follows from the evidence, but researchers have not tested its effects.

Conclusion

Methadone remains one of the most effective treatments for opioid addiction, reducing mortality and keeping people in care (Mattick et al., 2014; Sordo et al., 2017). For the large share of patients who also live with chronic pain, however, its strengths come with a built-in mismatch: a half-life that protects against withdrawal for a day and pain relief that fades within hours. The lowered pain threshold that long-term opioid exposure can produce, program rules built primarily to prevent diversion, and a single story that too often reads pain as drug-seeking compound that mismatch.

People with lifelong conditions such as spina bifida experience these problems in concentrated form, carrying several kinds of pain and a long history of opioid treatment into recovery. Meeting their needs does not require abandoning methadone. It requires scheduling it with its two clocks in mind, monitoring dose changes with patience, training counselors to assess pain rather than suspect it, and building communities in which patients' experience informs practice. The platform proposed here is one way to do that: a space in which the maps of pharmacology and the territory of lived experience meet, and in which counselors recognize people in recovery with pain as experts in their own lives.
